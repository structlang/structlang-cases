The noodle judgment is resolved. You chose a noodle shop and finished eating.

Now you face a bigger decision: you need to change jobs.

**Job A: Big Tech Company**

- High pay
- But 2-hour commute

**Job B: Nearby Small Company**

- Lower pay
- But walking distance

How to choose?

You try using line structure to judge:

**"High pay vs. low pay" axis:**

```
Low pay(-) ←—— 0 ——→ High pay(+)
  B(-0.3)           A(+0.8)
```

A is better.

**"Long commute vs. short commute" axis:**

```
Far(-) ←—— 0 ——→ Near(+)
  A(-0.7)         B(+0.9)
```

B is better.

**Two axes give contradictory conclusions.**

What should you do?

---

## The Single-Dimension Dilemma

If you only use one axis, you'll be stuck:

- Only look at pay → Choose A, but 4 hours daily commute will kill you
- Only look at commute → Choose B, but not enough money

Reality is: you need to **consider both** factors simultaneously.

Not first look at pay, then look at commute. Not finish looking at pay, then look at commute. Rather, **weigh both factors together**.

This is the problem surface structure solves.

---

## Two Axes Intersect

Put the two axes together:

```
           High pay(+)
              ↑
              |
              |  A(+,-)
              |  High pay but far
  Far(-) ←----O---------→ Near(+)
              |
              |  B(-,+)
              |  Low pay but near
              ↓
           Low pay(-)
```

Now, the two jobs are no longer two separate points on two axes, but two positions in a **two-dimensional space**:

- **Job A**: Located in (+, -) quadrant = High pay × Long distance
- **Job B**: Located in (-, +) quadrant = Low pay × Short distance

**This is surface structure.**

Notice the intersection point **O**—this isn't just any point.

**This is the shared origin of the two axes.**

What does this mean?

**Origins coinciding means both axes are under the same intention.**

In this example:

- The origin of the "high pay vs. low pay" axis is "acceptable or not" under the "choosing a job" intention
- The origin of the "long commute vs. short commute" axis is also "acceptable or not" under the "choosing a job" intention
- The two origins coincide, indicating they serve the same intention: "I want to choose a job"

If two axes' origins don't coincide, they cannot intersect to form a surface structure.

**A counterexample:**

Suppose you try to put "did my neighbor buy a TV" and "are the noodles tasty" together:

```
           Bought(+)
              ↑
              |
  Bad taste(-) ←---?--------→ Good taste(+)
              |
              ↓
           Didn't buy(-)
```

What is the intersection point?

- The origin of "bought or not" is judgment under the "want to know trivia" intention
- The origin of "noodles tasty or not" is judgment under the "eating lunch" intention

**The two origins don't coincide—these two axes cannot intersect to form a meaningful surface structure.**

You can forcibly draw this diagram, but it won't give you any useful decision-making information. Because you're using two different intentions to evaluate unrelated things.

---

## The Meaning of Four Quadrants

When two axes intersect, they form four regions:

```
     High pay(+)
        ↑
        |
(-, +)  |  (+, +)
Low pay |  High pay  ← Ideal
near    |  near
--------+--------→ Near(+)
High pay|  Low pay
far     |  far
(+, -)  |  (-, -)  ← Worst
        ↓
     Low pay(-)
```

**Four quadrants:**

**1. (++): High pay and near**

- Most ideal situation
- If such a job exists, choose directly

**2. (+-): High pay but far**

- Job A is here
- Has benefits, but has costs

**3. (-+): Low pay but near**

- Job B is here
- Lose some, but gain convenience

**4. (--): Low pay and far**

- Worst situation
- Eliminate directly

**Surface structure doesn't give you the answer—it gives you a decision map.**

You see:

- A is at (+-):: High benefit, high cost
- B is at (-+): Low benefit, low cost
- No perfect option (++)
- At least not the worst (--)

---

## Where Does the Tradeoff Happen?

Now the question is: Between A(+,-) and B(-,+), which to choose?

This depends on, under this intention, **which dimension matters more**.

**If you need money:**

- Pay has higher weight
- (+,-) though far, is worth it
- Choose A

**If you value quality of life:**

- Commute has higher weight
- (-,+) though less pay, is acceptable
- Choose B

**Surface structure doesn't decide for you, but it lets you see clearly: what kind of tradeoff you're making.**

---

## Not Two Judgments, But One Combined Judgment

Important: surface structure isn't "first judge pay, then judge commute."

Rather, **in the same instant, the brain simultaneously positions the object in both dimensions**.

When you see Job A, your brain doesn't:

1. First think "high pay, +0.8"
2. Then think "too far, -0.7"

Rather, it directly forms a **combined impression**:

- "High pay but sacrificing commute time"
- This is a holistic feeling, not two separate judgments

**This is why surface structure is a new level, not just simple addition of two lines.**

---

## Intention Must Be Unified

**First iron law of surface structure: Both axes must serve the same intention.**

Specifically:

**1. Origins must coincide**

- Both axes' origins anchor to the same intention
- This coinciding origin is the center of the surface structure

**2. Dimensions must be related**

- Both dimensions must affect decision-making under this intention
- "Pay" and "commute" both affect "choosing a job"
- "Pay" and "noodles tasty" don't affect the same decision

**3. Positive directions must be consistent**

- Positive directions of both axes point toward "better matches intention"
- Under "choosing a job" intention, "high pay" and "short commute" are both positive
- No contradiction like "high pay but I don't want money"

**Once origins don't coincide, surface structure collapses.**

This is why some decisions that seem to "consider multiple factors" are actually chaotic—because these factors come from different intentions, their origins aren't together at all.

For example:

"This job has high pay (job-choosing intention), but my mom doesn't like it (filial piety intention), and that city doesn't have good noodle shops (life enjoyment intention)."

This **looks like** a surface structure (multi-dimensional tradeoff), but is actually **three different line structures from different intentions fighting each other**.

Their origins don't coincide at all:

- "High pay" comes from "choosing a job" intention
- "Mom likes it or not" comes from "filial piety" intention
- "Has good noodles" comes from "life enjoyment" intention

You can forcibly draw a three-dimensional diagram, but it won't give you any useful decision-making information. Because you're using three different intentions to evaluate unrelated things.

**True surface structure requires: Same origin, same intention, simultaneously evaluating multiple related dimensions.**

---

## Surface Structure in Reality

Surface structure is everywhere.

**Investment decision:**

```
     High return(+)
        ↑
        |
Low risk |  High return low risk (++) Ideal
(-,+)    |  (+,+)
---------+--------→ Low risk(+)
High ret.|  Low return high risk
High risk|  (--) Worst
(+,-)    |  (-,-)
        ↓
     Low return(-)
```

**Choosing restaurant:**

```
     Tasty(+)
        ↑
        |
Expensive|  Cheap and tasty
but tasty|  (+,+)
(+,-)    |  
---------+--------→ Cheap(+)
Expensive|  Cheap but bad
and bad  |  tasting  
(--) Worst| (-,+)
        ↓
     Bad tasting(-)
```

**Evaluating people:**

```
     Capable(+)
        ↑
        |
Not cap. |  Capable and
but coop.|  cooperative
(-,+)    |  (++,) Ideal
---------+--------→ Cooperative(+)
Capable  |  Not capable not
not coop.|  cooperative
(+,-)    |  (--) Worst
        ↓
     Not capable(-)
```

Every time you consider two factors simultaneously, your brain automatically constructs surface structure.

---

## You've Always Been Using Surface Structure

Surface structure isn't a new invention—it's how your brain has always worked.

Every time you say:

- "This product is good, but a bit expensive" → (+,-)
- "This person isn't great, but has good attitude" → (-,+)
- "Cheap goods aren't good" → Exclude (-,+)
- "Good and cheap" → Seeking (++,)

You're using surface structure to make combined judgments.

What we're doing is simply **seeing this process clearly**:

- See clearly which two dimensions you're using
- See clearly which quadrant the object falls in
- See clearly what tradeoff you're making

Once you see clearly, many decisions are no longer chaotic:

- Why do different people make different decisions on the same choice → Different dimension weights
- Why are some decisions so difficult → Falls in (+,-) or (-,+), requires tradeoff
- Why don't people want some "good things" → Might be negative on another dimension

But surface structure isn't enough.

In reality, you often need to consider **three** factors simultaneously. For example, when choosing a job, you consider not just pay and commute, but also "development opportunities."

When two dimensions can already form judgment, your brain naturally thinks—'but what about the third?' This leads to the next level: Three-dimensional structure.
