## Introduction: From Observer to Designer

Last chapter, we learned how to **observe and analyze** external structures:

- Use third-party perspective to quickly judge power comparisons
- Identify how external structures affect my situation
- Formulate defensive, offensive, intervention strategies

But this is just the first step. The more advanced question is:

**Can I actively become a variable in others' structures?** **Can I design transmission chains to make others evolve in my expected direction?**

This chapter completes the crucial leap:

```
Chapter 14: Analyze external structures
    ↓
Chapter 15: Operate external structures
    ↓
From observer → designer
From passive response → active design
```

We'll show through a complete real case how to achieve long-term strategic goals through actively designing external structures.

## Section 1: Core Cognition—I Am an External Structure in Multiple Situations

### 1.1 Cognitive Transformation

**Traditional thinking:**

- External structures affect me
- I can only passively respond
- My choices: defend, attack, intervene, exit

**Operational thinking:**

- **My behavior also enters others' structures**
- I can actively design my behavior
- Through transmission chains, influence multi-party evolution

**Key insight:**

In any complex situation, **I simultaneously exist in multiple people's cognitive structures**:

- I'm an external structure for competitors (they must respond to me)
- I'm an external structure for customers (they must judge me)
- I'm an external structure for partners (they must evaluate me)

**My same behavior produces different transmission effects in different people's structures.**

### 1.2 I'm in Multiple People's Structures

When I take an action, this action simultaneously enters multiple people's cognitive structures:

|Whose structure am I in|What role am I|How is my behavior interpreted|
|---|---|---|
|Competitor|Pricing anchor|I raise price → they must respond (follow? maintain? lower?)|
|Potential customer|One option|My price → they judge value (expensive = good? or expensive = scam?)|
|Existing customer|Service provider|My quality + price → they judge whether worth it (loyal or leave)|
|Hesitators|Comparison standard|My reputation → they regret or rejoice (those who went to competitors)|
|Observers|Market signal|My existence → they judge market (is this industry good?)|

**Key insight:**

My **same behavior** (like raising price) produces **completely different effects** in different people's structures.

**This is the essence of external structure operation: design one behavior that simultaneously affects multiple structures, guiding them to evolve in my desired direction.**

## Section 2: Real Case—A Carefully Designed Game

### 2.1 Background and Dilemma

I once ran a children's training institution. In the same town, there were several competitors.

As latecomers, their strategy was clear: **anchor to my price, compete with low prices**.

Each time I priced 1000 yuan/term, they priced 800 yuan; I priced 1200 yuan, they priced 950 yuan. Using price advantage to grab customers.

I faced choices:

- Lower prices to follow? Fight a price war with them?
- Maintain unchanged? Keep original price, let customers flow away?
- Raise prices? This seems the most unreasonable choice...

At first, I also didn't know what to do.

### 2.2 My Thinking Process

I started seriously thinking about two questions: What if I lower prices? What if I raise prices?

#### If Lower Prices

Lowering prices can definitely benefit consumers, keep customers short-term.

But the problem is: **This town's student scale is limited.**

If I lower prices, opponents will continue to lower. Once price war starts, it's about who can last longer. Eventually both sides' profits compressed, no money to invest in quality, only getting worse.

This isn't the result I want.

#### If Raise Prices

Raising prices will bring economic pressure to parents, that's certain.

But I suddenly realized something deeper...

**Layer 1: Price-sensitive customers will be lost**

After raising prices, those parents extremely sensitive to price will leave, flow to opponents. This is inevitable.

**Layer 2: What kind of customers will opponents get?**

Here's a key cognition:

Generally price-extremely-sensitive customer groups may differ in educational investment and family cooperation level on average, which affects classroom atmosphere.

This isn't a value judgment, but statistical pattern.

**Layer 3: What will happen in opponents' classrooms?**

When opponents gain these customers, the proportion of "problem students" in classes will rise. Classroom discipline will worsen. Teachers hard to manage.

**Layer 4: What will parents think?**

Parents at opponents' place will most likely think: **"Maybe higher price is better?"**

Poor discipline → no effect → parents disappointed → regret not spending more money at better institution.

This chain of psychological activity will happen automatically.

**Layer 5: If they want to transfer to my place?**

When their children transfer to my training institution, **our classroom discipline and other aspects are better**.

Even if this child himself may not follow discipline in class, will restrain somewhat under group influence.

Good environment is self-reinforcing.

**Layer 6: Opponents' dilemma**

More critically: **Once my competitors choose low price, it's very hard to raise prices.**

Because parents will think: "I came here because it's cheap, and you raise it so much, I might as well go to another place."

Opponents locked in low price position, unable to move.

**Layer 7: Positive and negative cycles**

From here, divergence will appear:

My side:

- High fees → more resources → invest in quality
- Good quality → good reputation → can continue raising prices
- Positive cycle

Opponent's side:

- Low price → fewer resources → can only work on "quantity"
- Distribute flyers, advertise, open new projects
- But customer quality poor → reputation poor
- Negative cycle

**Layer 8: Matthew effect**

Over time, gap will widen.

**This creates a situation where the strong get stronger, the weak get weaker.**

After deducing this chain, I had a clear idea.

### 2.3 My Decision

Based on this deduction, I made a decision:

**Not only not lower prices, but continue to raise prices.**

Not a one-time large increase, but gradual small increases every few months. Each increase 10%-15%, letting customers gradually adapt.

Meanwhile, I invested increased revenue into things that truly accumulate:

- Better teachers
- More activities
- Better service

I was willing to bear short-term customer loss, waiting for mid-term effects to appear.

### 2.4 Actual Evolution

**The result was exactly as I predicted.**

#### Short-term (1-3 months)

After raising prices, indeed some parents left, flowed to competitors.

Opponents seemed to "benefit"—enrollment numbers rose, classrooms fuller.

#### Mid-term (3-6 months)

Opponents started encountering problems.

Feedback I heard:

- Their classroom discipline worsened
- Teachers complained students hard to manage
- Complaints in parent groups

While my side:

- Those remaining all valued quality
- Class atmosphere getting better and better
- Reputation started spreading

#### Long-term (6-12 months)

Opponents fell into a dilemma:

They wanted to improve quality, but lacked sufficient resources. They wanted to raise prices, but parents wouldn't accept. They were locked in low price position, unable to move.

While my side:

- Continued raising prices
- Continued investing in quality
- Positive cycle spinning faster

#### After 12 months

Divergence was already very obvious.

My investment direction:

- **Organize activities**: Improve experience, continuous accumulation
- **Hire good teachers**: Quality improvement, form reputation
- **Design UI, flyers, website**: Brand improvement, generate compound interest
- **Service as promotion**: Each service is promotion

Opponent's investment direction:

- **Constantly open new projects**: Hope to break through
- **Constantly increase flyer distribution**: Maintain exposure
- **Constantly increase ad investment and ad space occupation**: Compete for traffic

**Contrast is obvious:**

- My investment is accumulative (easier over time)
- Opponent's investment is consumptive (no effect if stopped)

I don't need to "constantly" try, reputation spreads automatically. Opponents must "constantly" try, because existing isn't enough.

**"Constantly" itself is proof of weakness.**

### 2.5 Final Balance

Now, situation is already very clear.

Opponents **barely maintained weak position**.

Although I think competitors are always a threat, **I'm happy to see this balance**.

Why?

Because:

- Opponent's existence highlights my value (comparison)
- Opponent's "cheap" positioning makes them hard to change
- Opponent's "barely maintaining" prevents new competitors from entering

I don't need to completely eliminate opponents. **Control is more valuable than elimination.**

## Section 3: Structural Analysis of Transmission Chain

Now let's re-interpret this case with StructLang, extracting replicable methodology.

### 3.1 Complete Deconstruction of Transmission Chain

The 8-layer transmission in the training institution case can be precisely described with StructLang:

**Starting structure (T0):**

```
Me: Sufficient resources, good quality, high pricing (superior +)
Opponent: Average resources, just started, low pricing (subordinate -)
Structure type: +- (imbalance structure, I have advantage)
```

**Trigger action:** I raise price

**Layer 1 transmission: Customer diversion**

```
Price-sensitive customers' perspective:
- Me: Too expensive (--)
- Opponent: Cheap (++)
→ Customers choose opponent
```

**Layer 2 transmission: Opponent gains low-quality customer base**

```
Opponent's structure change:
- Customer quantity: Increase (+)
- Customer quality: Decline (-)
- Overall: +- (Surface growth, actually hidden danger)
```

**Layer 3 transmission: Opponent's teaching environment deteriorates**

```
Opponent internal:
- Proportion of poorly disciplined students rises
- Teacher management difficulty increases
- Teaching quality declines
Structure type: -- (Resources and action both blocked)
```

**Layer 4 transmission: Parent disappointment and regret**

```
Parents at opponent's place:
- Expectation: + (Cheap, also expect good)
- Reality: - (Poor discipline, no effect)
- Psychology: Regret, "you get what you pay for"
```

**Layer 5 transmission: Comparison psychology strengthening**

```
Parents' re-evaluation of me:
- Originally: Too expensive (-)
- Now: Worth the money (+)
- Action: Want to transfer to my place
```

**Layer 6 transmission: Opponent's path locking**

```
Opponent's dilemma:
- Customer expectation: "This place is cheap"
- If raise price: Customer backlash
- Cannot raise price: Resource scarcity
Structure type: Fall into negative cycle (--)
```

**Layer 7 transmission: Positive-negative cycle divergence**

```
My positive cycle:
High fees(+) → Invest in quality(+) → Good reputation(+) → Continue raising price(+)
     ↑_____________________________________↓

Opponent's negative cycle:
Low price(-) → Few resources(-) → Poor quality(-) → Poor reputation(-) → Harder to raise price(-)
   ↑_____________________________________↓
```

**Layer 8 transmission: Matthew effect**

```
Final structure (T12):
Me: ++ (Stable win-win, with quality customers)
Opponent: -- (On edge of collapse, barely maintaining)
Gap: From +- to ++/--, complete divergence
```

### 3.2 Identification of Key Nodes

In this transmission chain, three key nodes determine the entire structure's evolution:

#### Locking point: Opponent's pricing trap (Layer 6)

```
Opponent chooses low price
    ↓
Customer expectation locked
    ↓
Cannot raise price
    ↓
[Irreversible dilemma]
```

**Characteristics:**

- This is an irreversible effect
- Once customer expectation forms, opponents hard to change
- This is the "trap" I designed

#### Cycle point: Positive-negative cycle divergence (Layer 7)

```
My positive cycle: Quality → Reputation → Raise price → Resources → Quality
                ↑_________________↓

Opponent's negative cycle: Low price → Few resources → Poor quality → Poor reputation → Low price
                ↑__________________↓
```

**Characteristics:**

- This is self-reinforcing effect
- Positive cycle spins faster, negative cycle sinks deeper
- Time is my friend, opponent's enemy

#### Waiting point: Parent disappointment accumulation (Layer 4)

```
Short-term: Parents expect cheap
    ↓ (needs 3-6 months)
Mid-term: Parents experience poor
    ↓ (needs patient waiting)
Long-term: Parents regret
```

**Characteristics:**

- This effect has time delay
- Needs waiting to appear
- This is why I must bear short-term loss

### 3.3 Why This Design Works

From StructLang perspective, why this design works:

**1. Utilizes conditional dependence of structure**

Customer quality isn't fixed, but **result of price screening**:

- High price screening → customers valuing quality → good environment
- Low price screening → price-sensitive customers → poor environment

**2. Creates path dependence trap**

Opponent's "low price" choice creates path dependence:

- Low price → customer expectation → hard to raise price → resource scarcity
- Each step seems "reasonable", but overall is trap

**3. Designs time difference advantage**

```
Short-term: I lose (customer loss)
Mid-term: Opponent's problems appear (environment deteriorates)
Long-term: I harvest (positive cycle accelerates)
```

Willing to bear short-term loss, exchange for long-term advantage.

**4. Utilizes multi-party linkage**

My one action (raise price), simultaneously affects:

- Opponent's customer structure
- Opponent's teaching environment
- Parents' psychological expectations
- Market's overall pattern

One stone stirs a thousand waves.

## Section 4: Operational Methodology—Transmission Chain Design

Based on case analysis, we can extract replicable transmission chain design methods.

### 4.1 Design Process: Five-Step Method

**Step 1: List all affected objects**

Training institution case:

- Competitors
- Price-sensitive customers
- Quality-valuing customers
- Customers at opponent's place
- Potential observers

**Framework expression:**

```
[Affected Object List]

Direct objects:
□ Competitors
□ Existing customers
□ Potential customers

Indirect objects:
□ Opponent's customers
□ Market observers
□ Other ___
```

**Step 2: Deduce transmission chain for each object (at least 3 layers)**

|Object|Layer 1 effect|Layer 2 effect|Layer 3 effect|...|
|---|---|---|---|---|
|Opponent|Gains my lost customers|Customer quality declines|Teaching quality declines|...|
|Potential customer|Thinks I'm expensive|Goes to opponent|Poor experience|...|
|Existing customer|Price increase|But better service|Satisfaction improves|...|
|Hesitator|Hears comparison|Regrets going to opponent|Wants to transfer to my place|...|

**Step 3: Identify key nodes**

**Locking point**: Which effect is irreversible?

- Training institution case: Opponent chooses low price → customer expectation locked

**Cycle point**: Which effect is self-reinforcing?

- Training institution case: Quality → reputation → raise price → resources → quality

**Waiting point**: Which effect has time delay?

- Training institution case: Parent disappointment needs 3-6 months accumulation

**Step 4: Design branch point strategy**

At key nodes, opponents may have multiple choices. When designing, ensure: **No matter which path opponent chooses, I have advantage.**

Training institution case branch points:

```
Opponent faces choice:

Path A: Lower price to follow
    → Price war, I have more resources, last longer

Path B: Maintain original price
    → Gap widens, I continue leading

Path C: Follow raise price
    → Customers question "why should you", lose only advantage

Path D: Improve quality
    → Insufficient resources, hard to achieve

[All paths favor me]
```

**Step 5: Verify robustness**

**Hypothesis testing:**

|Hypothesis scenario|My response|Still have advantage|
|---|---|---|
|Opponent crazily lowers price|Maintain price, let opponent exhaust themselves|✓|
|Customers completely don't flow back|Remaining customers are quality customers|✓|
|New competitor appears|Opponent's "barely maintaining" prevents new entrants|✓|
|Opponent suddenly collapses|May have new competitors, but I've established barriers|✓|
|Overall market shrinks|My customers higher quality, more able to sustain|✓|

**Robustness standards:**

- In most cases, I have advantage
- In worst case, I also won't fail
- Have sufficient adjustment space

### 4.2 Three Core Principles

#### Principle 1: Multi-layer transmission (at least 5 layers)

**Wrong approach:**

**Correct approach:**


**Operation points:**

#### Principle 2: Trade time for space

**Core cognition:**

Strategic operation inevitably has time difference:

```
Short-term: I bear loss
Mid-term: Transmission appears
Long-term: I harvest advantage
```

**Training institution case:**

- Short-term (1-3 months): Customer loss 20%, revenue decline
- Mid-term (3-6 months): Opponent's problems appear, reputation comparison
- Long-term (6-12 months): Positive cycle accelerates, gap widens

**Without patience to wait, can't see long-term effect.**

**Operation points:**

- Set time expectations in advance
- Prepare sufficient resource buffer
- Maintain resolve during short-term loss
- Set monitoring nodes, verify transmission as expected

#### Principle 3: Internal cultivation priority

**Two resource allocation types:**

|Type|Characteristics|Examples|Effect|
|---|---|---|---|
|Accumulative investment|Continuous growth, form compound interest|Quality, brand, reputation, team capability|Easier over time|
|Consumptive investment|Linear effect, disappears when stopped|Ads, promotions, flyers, temporary activities|Must continuously invest|

**Training institution case comparison:**

My investment:

- Activities → experience accumulation
- Good teachers → quality improvement
- UI/website → brand assets
- Service → automatic spread

Opponent's investment:

- Flyers → instant exposure
- Ads → rental effect
- New projects → trial and error cost

**Time is friend of accumulative investment, enemy of consumptive investment.**

|Improve quality (action)|Competition pressure (action)|Expectation rises (attitude)|
|Reduce promotion (action)|Competition space (action)|Mystery (attitude)|
|Disclose data (quality)|Transparency pressure (quality)|Trust rises (attitude)|

#### Operation 2: Create structural event

**Definition:** Discrete, with clear nodes change, not continuous adjustment

**Function:**

```

    ↓
Make opponent realize "structure has changed"
    ↓
Trigger opponent's active re-judgment
```

**Training institution case (optimized version):**

If I had done:

- **Announce price increase plan in advance** (create expectation)
- **Set last discount period** (create urgency)
- **Disclose customer choice reasons** (create conformity)

Effect would be stronger.

But even without explicitly creating events, continuous price increases themselves are structural signals.

**Comparison:**

|Method|Effect|Transmission speed|Controllability|
|---|---|---|---|
|Continuous adjustment|Opponent may not notice|Slow|High|
|Create event|Opponent must notice and re-judge|Fast|Medium|

**Operation principles:**

1. Important changes should be made into "events"
2. Events should have clear time nodes
3. Events should be observable, spreadable
4. Events should be followed up and reinforced

**Mechanism:** I change → affect A → A affects B → B affects C → feedback to me

## Section 5: Opponent Response and Balance Control

### 5.1 Framework for Predicting Opponent Reactions

When designing transmission chains, must predict opponent's possible reactions.

**Opponent's choice space in training institution case:**

```
Opponent observes: I raise price, some customers flow to them

Opponent's choices:

Option A: Lower price to follow
    → If choose A, I maintain price, let opponent exhaust themselves
    
Option B: Maintain original price  
    → If choose B, I continue improving quality, widen gap
    
Option C: Follow raise price
    → If choose C, I emphasize investment and quality, opponent can't match
    
Option D: Try new projects
    → If choose D, I focus on existing projects, opponent resources scattered
```

**My core strategy: No matter what opponent chooses, I just do my own quality well.**

**This is "no move defeats all moves"—not targeting opponent's moves, but strengthening my own structure.**

### 5.2 Opponent's Structural Dilemma

**Why does opponent "constantly" try but can't break through?**

From StructLang analysis, opponent falls into **triple dilemma**:

#### Dilemma 1: Path locking

```
Structure evolution:
Low price positioning (T0)
    ↓
Customer expectation forms (T3): "This place is cheap"
    ↓
Want to raise price (T6) → customer backlash: "Why should you raise?"
    ↓
Cannot raise price (T9) → resource scarcity → quality can't improve
    ↓
[Locked in low-end, hard to turn around]
```

**Characteristics:** Path dependence, irreversible

#### Dilemma 2: Customer quality trap

```
Low price attracts price-sensitive customers
    ↓
These customers: Demanding, low loyalty, high management cost
    ↓
Service cost higher than expected
    ↓
Even lower profit
    ↓
Harder to improve
    ↓
[Fall into vicious cycle]
```

**Characteristics:** Customer quality reversely affects operating cost

#### Dilemma 3: Diminishing marginal returns

```
Invest in ads, flyers, new projects
    ↓
First time: Obvious effect
Second time: Effect halved
Third time: Even worse effect
    ↓
Must continuously increase investment
    ↓
Efficiency getting lower
    ↓
Resource consumption
    ↓
[Accumulative scarcity]
```

**Characteristics:** Fate of consumptive investment

**Structural analysis:**

```
Opponent's dilemma structure:
- Path locking (irreversible)
- Customer trap (negative cycle)
- Diminishing marginal returns (accumulative scarcity)

Triple dilemma overlay → almost unsolvable
```

### 5.3 Methods to Maintain Favorable Balance

**Core cognition: Control opponent in "weak but existing" state.**

#### Why not completely eliminate opponent?

**Reason 1: Comparison value**

```
Opponent exists and obviously worse
    ↓
Highlights my value
    ↓
Customers more easily understand "why I'm expensive"
    ↓
My pricing more easily accepted
```

**Reason 2: Market education**

```
Opponent also doing market education
    ↓
Training's value, category awareness
    ↓
I don't need to bear this cost alone
    ↓
Reduce my customer acquisition cost
```

**Reason 3: Prevent new entrants**

```
If market looks "very easy to do"
    ↓
Attracts new competitors
    ↓
Opponent's "barely maintaining"
    ↓
Makes market look "not easy to do"
    ↓
Newcomers deterred
```

**Reason 4: Maintain vigilance**

```
Completely no competition
    ↓
Easy to slack off
    ↓
Threat exists
    ↓
Maintain improvement motivation
```

#### Operational methods to control balance

**Strategy 1: Don't actively attack**

- Don't lower price to squeeze opponent
- Don't malicious competition
- Maintain "gentleman's competition"

**Strategy 2: Maintain moderate gap**

- Continuously improve quality
- But not crushing advantage
- Let opponent "have hope but can't reach"

**Strategy 3: Occasionally give space**

- Don't compete in certain channels
- Let opponent "barely maintain"
- For example: I don't do certain low-price projects

**Strategy 4: Dynamic monitoring**

- Observe opponent state
- Prevent complete collapse (lose reference)
- Prevent strong counterattack (threaten balance)

**Control range:**

```
Opponent state spectrum:

Collapse ←---- Barely maintain ----→ Strong competition
      ↑
    [Optimal control point]
    
Too weak: Lose comparison value
Too strong: Threaten my position
```

**Not applicable to:**

- Complete information symmetry situations
- Opponent doesn't do structure judgment (pure random decisions)
- Time window extremely short can't wait for transmission

### 6.4 Verification Checklist

Before executing external structure operation, use this checklist for self-check:

```
[Operation Design Verification Checklist]

□ Transmission chain analysis
  □ Have I deduced at least 5 layers?
  □ Have I identified locking point, cycle point, waiting point?
  □ Have I considered multiple objects?

□ Opponent response
  □ Have I predicted opponent's possible reactions?
  □ Do I have response for each reaction?
  □ Do I have "no-move" strategy (advantage regardless of opponent)?

□ Time design
  □ Do I have sufficient resource buffer to bear short-term loss?
  □ Have I set clear time expectations?
  □ Have I set monitoring nodes?

□ Resource allocation
  □ Am I prioritizing accumulative resources?
  □ Am I avoiding over-reliance on consumptive investment?
  □ Can I form compound interest effect?

□ Robustness
  □ In worst case, will I still not fail?
  □ Do I have sufficient adjustment space?
  □ Can I make mid-course corrections?

□ Balance control
  □ Have I considered value of maintaining balance?
  □ Have I designed mechanism to control opponent state?
  □ Have I avoided "driving to extinction"?
```
