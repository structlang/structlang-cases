## A Puzzling Phenomenon

The company project is delayed.

**Operations Department's explanation**: "Because the tech team didn't deliver on time."

**Tech Team's explanation**: "Because requirements kept changing, we simply couldn't finish."

**Product Manager's explanation**: "Because the market changes so fast, we had to adjust direction."

**Boss's explanation**: "Because everyone doesn't cooperate well, there are communication problems."

Four explanations, all "make sense." Each person has reasons and evidence, logic is self-consistent.

But they can't all be simultaneously "right"—at least on the question "how to avoid delays next time," these four logics point to completely different action plans.

**What happened?**

---

## Logic Isn't Discovery but Matching

Back to Chapter 13, we discussed reason matching: thinking isn't "discovering truth" but "matching a reasonable explanation from available materials."

Now we can say it more directly: **Logic is the systematized version of reason matching.**

Operations, tech team, product, boss—they're not "discovering" the real cause of the delay but:

- Based on their respective **intentions** (accountability/self-protection/explanation/management)
- From their respective **experience libraries** (causal impressions)
- Matching an explanation that **makes sense** in the current context

**This is how logic actually operates.**

It's not some mysterious "rational thinking," nor some objective "truth discovery," but simply:

**Under specific intention, based on available materials, matching a self-consistent explanation or path.**

## From Point to Reasoning: This Book Is a Complete Logic System

We can now look back at the entire book.

From Chapter 1's point structure to Chapter 18, we've actually been discussing the same thing: **how humans think**.

And this "how to think" is a complete logic system.

### First Layer: Intention and Manifestation

**Chapter 1** discusses: **Intention determines what manifests**.

You're hungry, the spicy hot pot sign "pops" out. You're not hungry, it recedes into the background.

This is logic's **starting point**:

- Without intention, there's no thinking direction
- Different intentions mean different available "materials"

Operations' intention is "accountability," so the point "tech delay" manifests. Tech team's intention is "self-protection," so the point "requirement changes" manifests.

**Same delay event, different intentions, different manifestations.**

This isn't about "who sees more comprehensively" but that **logic necessarily starts from specific intention**.

### Second Layer: Judgment and Origin

**Chapter 2** discusses: **Intention determines judgment's positive-negative direction**.

Is watermelon sweet?

- You want to eat: sweet (+)
- Diabetic person: sweet (-)

Same "sweet," under different intentions, positive-negative direction completely opposite.

This is logic's **reference frame**:

- Every judgment has an origin
- Origin is determined by intention
- Different origins mean different judgments

Operations' origin is "deliver on promise," so tech not delivering is (-). Tech team's origin is "stable requirements," so requirement changes are (-).

**Not about "who's more objective," but origins are inherently different.**

### Third Layer: Combination and Tradeoff

**Chapter 3** discusses: **How to combine multiple judgments**.

Job A: High pay (+), but long commute (-). Job B: Low pay (-), but short commute (+).

This is logic's **multi-dimensional processing**:

- Real problems often involve multiple dimensions
- Different dimensions may give contradictory conclusions
- Need to position across multiple dimensions simultaneously

Delay problem is the same:

- Technical capability dimension: Tech team (+)
- Requirements management dimension: Product (-)
- Communication collaboration dimension: Entire team (-)

**Logic isn't a single axis but multi-dimensional space.**

### Fourth Layer: Compression and Decompression

**Chapters 8, 11** discuss: **Information must be compressed**.

I tell Mom "dog returned," she understands as "not keeping pets anymore." I tell colleague the detailed process, he understands as "caught the key point."

This is logic's **information processing mechanism**:

- You can't transmit all information
- Listener decompresses based on their own intention and experience
- Decompression isn't "restoration" but "reconstruction"

Operations says "tech delay," listener might decompress as "tech is incompetent." Tech says "requirements change," listener might decompress as "product is unprofessional."

**Logical reasoning is essentially a cycle of compression-decompression-recompression.**

### Fifth Layer: Causality and Matching

**Chapters 9, 13** discuss: **How to find "because"**.

Why the delay?

Not "discovering" an objectively existing cause, but:

- In your causal impression library (experience)
- Finding a connection that can explain the current result
- This is the answer to "why"

Operations' causal impression: "Tech not delivering→Project delay" Tech's causal impression: "Requirements chaotic changes→Can't finish→Delay" Product's causal impression: "Market changes→Must adjust→Delay"

**Every reason is "correct" because in their respective experience libraries, they're high-frequency verified stable connections.**

What to do?

Also not "searching" for the one correct method, but:

- Based on your intention (avoid next delay)
- From your causal impression library
- Matching a feasible path

Operations' solution: Strengthen supervision of tech team Tech's solution: Lock requirements, don't allow changes Product's solution: Establish rapid response mechanism

**Every solution is "feasible" but based on different causal assumptions.**

### Sixth Layer: Leap and Correction

**Chapter 7** discusses: **Logic can suddenly change**.

In the fake dog return process:

- T0-T2: Commercial logic (Merchant dishonest→Deadlock)
- After T2: Legal logic (Merchant illegal→Can apply pressure)

This is logic's **correction mechanism**:

- New information appears
- Original logic fails
- Leaps to new logic

Delay problem can also leap: Boss discovers the biggest problem isn't tech or product but that the team isn't working under a unified intention at all.

**Logic isn't monolithic but can leap according to circumstances.**

## So, What Is Logic?

**Logic = Under intention drive, based on available materials (knowledge, experience, causal impressions), through compression-decompression mechanism, matching a self-consistent explanation or path, and a cognitive system that corrects with new information.**

Breaking it down:

**Intention-driven**:

- Determines what manifests (Chapter 1)
- Determines where origin is (Chapter 2)
- Determines what to optimize (Chapter 10)

**Based on available materials**:

- Knowledge structure (what you've learned)
- Experience structure (what you've experienced)
- Causal impressions (what connections you've formed)

**Compression-decompression mechanism**:

- Can't process all information
- Must compress into operable "points"
- Decompress when needed into "line/surface/volume"
- Recompress when transmitting to others

**Match self-consistent path**:

- Not "discovering truth"
- But "finding reasons that make sense"
- Match backward = Explain "why"
- Match forward = Plan "what to do"

**Correct with new information**:

- Original logic may fail
- Need to leap to new logic
- This isn't "error" but normal correction

**This is what the book has been discussing from start to finish: a complete logic system.**

## Comparison with Traditional Logic

You may have learned traditional logic: major premise, minor premise, conclusion.

- All humans will die (major premise)
- Socrates is human (minor premise)
- Therefore Socrates will die (conclusion)

This is **formal logic**. It focuses on: if premises are true, the **reasoning form** that makes conclusions necessarily true.

**Structural language doesn't deny formal logic's value**, but it reveals questions formal logic hasn't answered:

### Three Blind Spots of Formal Logic

**Blind Spot 1: Where does the major premise come from?**

Formal logic says: "All humans will die" is the major premise. But where did this major premise come from?

**Structural language's answer:** This is a causal impression, coming from:

- Countless observations of co-occurrence
- Never seeing someone who doesn't die
- Compressed into stable connection "human→death"

**Major premise doesn't fall from the sky but is product of experience compression.**

**Blind Spot 2: How to choose the major premise?**

Facing complex problems, there can be many major premises:

- "All living things will die"
- "All organisms will decay"
- "All matter will change"

Which to choose? Formal logic doesn't answer.

**Structural language's answer:** Determined by intention:

- Your intention is "predict lifespan" → Choose "human→death"
- Your intention is "study biological laws" → Choose "living thing→death"
- Your intention is "philosophical thinking" → Choose "matter→change"

**Intention determines which major premise you invoke.**

**Blind Spot 3: How does conclusion guide action?**

Conclude "Socrates will die," then what?

Formal logic stops here.

**Structural language's answer:** Still need to match forward:

- Intention is "cherish time" → Action: Seize the present
- Intention is "inherit thought" → Action: Record dialogues
- Intention is "accept reality" → Action: Don't resist death

**From conclusion to action still requires reason matching mechanism.**

### Relationship Between Two Types of Logic

**Formal logic:**

- Focuses on validity of reasoning form
- Tool value: Check if reasoning is self-consistent

**Structural language:**

- Focuses on reasoning's generation process
- Tool value: Understand how thinking happens

**Not replacement but complementarity.**

Formal logic tells you: Whether "if A and B, then C" is valid. Structural language tells you:

- Why you chose A and B as premises
- Where A and B come from
- How to guide action after deriving C

### Example: Project Delay Problem

**Formal logic analysis:**

- Major premise: Requirement changes→Can't finish
- Minor premise: Requirements indeed changed
- Conclusion: Therefore can't finish

Reasoning is valid.

**Structural language's depth:**

- Why choose "requirement changes" this premise? (Intention: Tech dept self-protection)
- What's the quality of this causal impression? (Validation frequency, scope of application)
- What about other premises? ("Insufficient tech capability" can also explain)
- How does conclusion guide action? (Lock requirements vs. Improve capability)

**Combining both is complete logical analysis.**

## Why Unify Understanding of Logic?

You might ask: Since everyone has their own logic, why still unify understanding?

Because:

### 1. Understand Sources of Misunderstanding

On the delay problem, operations and tech team don't have "who's right who's wrong," but:

- Different intentions (accountability vs. self-protection)
- Different origins (deliver on promise vs. stable requirements)
- Different causal impressions (tech→delay vs. requirements→delay)

**Once you understand this, you know: They're fundamentally arguing in different logic systems.**

Solution isn't "who persuades whom" but:

- Clarify common intention (how to avoid next delay)
- Anchor common origin (what are project success standards)
- Compare validation frequency of causal impressions (which experience is more reliable)

### 2. Improve Logic Quality

Though "every reason is correct," reasons have quality levels.

**Judgment criteria**:

- **Validation frequency**: How many times has this causal connection been verified?
- **Scope of application**: Effective in how many situations?
- **Prediction accuracy**: Can it accurately predict results?

Operations says "tech not delivering→delay," what's this causal impression's quality?

- Validation frequency: Perhaps only this once
- Scope of application: Only holds when "requirements stable"
- Prediction accuracy: Can't predict if next time will be on time

Tech says "requirements chaotic changes→delay," what's this quality?

- Validation frequency: May have experienced multiple times
- Scope of application: Holds in multiple projects
- Prediction accuracy: Can predict: Stable requirements will be on time

**Different reason quality means different logic reliability.**

### 3. Actively Design Logic

Understanding logic's mechanism, you can:

**Actively choose intention**: Not working under "accountability" intention but under "improvement" intention.

**Actively anchor origin**: Not using respective origins but using commonly recognized origins.

**Actively invoke causal impressions**: Not using low-quality impressions but using high-quality, verified impressions.

**Actively design leaps**: When discovering current logic doesn't work, actively introduce new information, trigger leaps.

**This isn't "manipulating logic" but "engineering logic usage."**

## Common Sense: Logic's Bottom Line

Speaking of this, must discuss common sense.

Many people think common sense is "shallow," think knowledge is "advanced." This is misunderstanding.

**Let's distinguish three types of structures:**

### Experience-Type Structure: Individual's Immediate Compression

- Yesterday's meal you ate
- Last week's person you met
- Today's road you walked

**Characteristics:**

- Highly personalized
- Difficult to transfer across contexts
- Validation frequency: Once or few times
- Scope of application: Specific spacetime

### Knowledge-Type Structure: Group's Abstract Framework

- Newton's laws of motion
- Economic principles
- Programming language syntax

**Characteristics:**

- Remove individual details
- Can transfer across contexts
- Validation frequency: Multiple times (but requires professional training)
- Scope of application: Specific domains

### Common Sense-Type Structure: Ultra-High-Frequency Verified Bottom-Line Rules

- Don't hand over all chips
- Be vigilant when promises are too perfect
- People change under extreme pressure

**Characteristics:**

- Cross-cultural, cross-spacetime
- Validation frequency: Billions of people × Thousands of years
- Scope of application: Almost all contexts
- Failure cost: Usually fatal

**Common sense isn't "shallow knowledge" but "ultra-high-frequency verified bottom-line rules."**

Its quality is manifested in:

- **Highest validation frequency**: Countless people verified in countless contexts
- **Broadest scope of application**: Almost all cultures and eras
- **Highest failure cost**: Violating common sense is often fatal

### Common Sense Example Analysis

"Don't hand over all chips"—this is common sense.

Why is this high-quality causal impression?

**Validation frequency:**

- Countless people verified in business, politics, relationships, war
- Violators almost inevitably fail

**Scope of application:**

- In negotiations
- In investments
- In relationships
- In war
- Almost all game scenarios

**Prediction accuracy:**

- Once you hand over all chips, you lose correction ability
- Other party can unilaterally change rules
- You become completely passive object

**This is why common sense is logic's bottom line.**

When your reasoning conflicts with common sense, it's not "common sense is outdated" but:

- Your intention may have been manipulated
- Your origin may have been swapped
- Your causal impressions may be low quality

### Beware "Logic" That Makes You Abandon Common Sense

**"Give everything to the organization, organization will protect you."** ← Makes you hand over chips

**"Truly loving me means unconditional support."** ← Makes you hand over judgment authority

**"If you don't buy this, you're not a successful person."** ← Rewrites your origin

**Using common sense to test logic is the final insurance.**

Theory finished, how to use?

- What do they want to achieve?
- What do I want to achieve?
- Is there common intention?

**Step Two: Clarify Each Party's Origin**

- What do they use as reference?
- What do I use as reference?
- Can we anchor common origin?

**Step Three: Compare Each Party's Causal Impressions**

- What experience are they based on?
- What experience am I based on?

**Step Four: Seek Common Logic**

- Can we work under common intention?
- Can we use high-quality causal impressions?

**Step One: Clarify Your Intention**

- What situation indicates this logic is wrong?

## Boundaries and Honesty

Finally, what are this understanding's limitations?

### 1. Doesn't Solve Value Judgments

"Should tech work overtime to catch up?"

This logic system can tell you:

- Possible consequences of overtime (employee fatigue, quality decline)
- Possible consequences of not overtime (project continues delay, client dissatisfaction)

But it can't tell you: Which "should" be chosen.

Because "should" depends on your values:

- Do you value employee welfare more or client satisfaction?
- Do you value short-term efficiency more or long-term quality?

**Logic can only help you see choices' costs, can't make value judgments for you.**

### 2. Doesn't Guarantee Prediction Accuracy

Even if your causal impression quality is high, predictions can still fail.

Because:

- There may be variables you don't know
- Low-probability events may occur
- Structural leaps may happen

**Logic can only improve prediction probability, can't guarantee absolute accuracy.**

### 3. Can't Completely Eliminate Misunderstandings

Even if you clarified intention, anchored origin, used high-quality causal impressions, communication can still be misunderstood.

Because:

- Your compression ≠ Other's decompression
- Other party has their own intentions and experiences
- Language itself is limited

**Logic can only reduce misunderstandings, can't completely eliminate them.**
